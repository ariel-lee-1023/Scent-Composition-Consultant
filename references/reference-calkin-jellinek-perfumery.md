## Mental Model (read first)
A perfume is an identifiable, balanced structure that performs through time in a particular product. Knowing pleasant materials is insufficient: the perfumer must learn relationships, recognize patterns, compare trials, and distinguish odor strength, diffusion, persistence, and stability. The authors connect compositional apprenticeship to physical chemistry and psychophysics without making an analytical instrument the judge of aesthetic success.

## Frameworks & Structure
### Chapters 9–10: The Structure of a Perfume; The Use of Bases
- **Perfumery accord**: a combination that acquires a recognizable character beyond its ingredients. Explore two materials at several ratios before adding complexity. The authors' 9:1 through 1:9 exercise searches for an aesthetically convincing relation, often comparable perceived intensity, rather than equal weights. Similar volatility is a useful starting condition, not a rule forbidding combinations across evaporation rates.
- **Identity through evaporation**: a fine fragrance should retain a recognizable organizing idea as its balance changes. Strength, diffusion, persistence, and chemical stability are separate requirements. A persistent material can remain close to the substrate; a loud opening does not prove an effective late phase.
- **Simplicity and complexity**: a few major components can carry identity while minor components supply nuance. Additional ingredients must contribute to the whole or to its internal relationships. Repeatedly adding pleasant ingredients without a structural purpose is not composition.
- **Bases**: prefabricated subcompositions, from small accords to complex mixtures. They can deliver character and otherwise inconvenient traces. Several bases may duplicate the same ingredients, obscure diagnosis, complicate production, and inflate cost. Distinguish a compositional base from base notes and from the unperfumed product base.
- **Open formula versus bases**: neither is intrinsically superior. Open formulas make matching and diagnosis more transparent; a well-chosen base can supply a distinctive effect economically. Trace complexity hidden inside a base means a short ingredient list need not be a chemically simple formula.

### Chapters 1–5: Basic Skills and Techniques
- **Discrimination versus sensitivity**: exceptional detection sensitivity is not equivalent to compositional competence. Recognition, discrimination, memory, concentration, aesthetic judgment, and realistic collaboration matter. A material-specific perceptual gap is not evidence that someone cannot learn perfumery.
- **Technique of smelling**: use controlled diluted samples, consistent blotters, a relatively odor-free evaluation area, brief attentive sampling, and time away from the stimulus. Let the initial solvent impression pass. Avoid comparing a freshly wet strip with an aged reference or allowing laboratory background odor to become an invisible confound.
- **Learning and classification of raw materials**: associate a material's identity with its odor family, specific character, behavior through time, and relationship to other materials. Names provide retrieval cues, but performance notes must come from the actual sample and application. Trade names, natural extracts, and chemically defined substances are different kinds of identification.
- **Raw-material origin**: distillation and extraction produce different aromatic objects; an absolute is not automatically an intact copy of a living flower's emitted odor. Grade, processing, storage, and supplier matter. A natural material's complexity can be useful, but also makes substitutions and batch consistency harder.

### Chapter 6: The Floral Accords
The compounding tables distinguish a basic structure from selected variants and modifiers. They are historical learning maps, not ready-to-use product formulas or current permitted-material lists.

| Effect | Structural anchors in the book | Direction of variation |
|---|---|---|
| Rose | Phenylethyl alcohol with rose alcohols such as citronellol, geraniol, and nerol | Esters, green facets, ionones, and high-impact rose modifiers alter freshness, fruitiness, or depth; they do not all have equal potency. |
| Jasmine | Benzyl acetate, floral alcohols, linalool, and jasminic materials | Indolic, anthranilate, green, and spicy facets distinguish a fresh floral sketch from an absolute-like or more animalic impression. |
| Violet | Ionones and methyl ionones | Leaf-green materials and woody or powdery support change leaf versus flower emphasis. A violet leaf extract is not interchangeable with a violet-flower illusion. |
| Neroli / orange blossom | Phenylethyl alcohol, linalool, petitgrain and related fresh facets | Anthranilate and indolic accents move toward richer blossom; overly forceful accents can obscure the fresh structure. |

Other floral variants are compressed into the same basic-structure/modifier method. Some names in the supplied conversion have broken font encoding; uncertain table entries and numerical formulas are not reconstructed by guesswork.

### Chapter 7: The Technique of Matching
- **Smelling in patterns**: identify an overall family or familiar configuration, then attend to its departures. This is a hypothesis generator. Recognizing a fougère does not analytically establish that every classical ingredient is present.
- **Analytical and comparative smelling**: examine early and late phases, direct attention to a proposed facet, then compare a trial against the target. Dry-out can expose components masked at the opening. Compare matched sample age, dilution, and substrate.
- **Conservative matching**: begin with clearly supported elements. A wrong component is harder to undo than a missing one is to add. Keep uncertain materials out of the first reconstruction, preserve a control, and record why each new trial differs.
- **Tunnel smelling**: fixation on one feature can make the remaining composition disappear from attention. Step back, smell the whole, return after a break, or obtain another evaluation before chasing an increasingly narrow mismatch.
- **Instrument-assisted matching**: chromatography can reveal composition; olfactory relevance and a convincing reconstruction still require smelling. A peak list does not determine the unique formula or aesthetic role of each constituent.

### Chapters 8, 11–12: Aesthetics and Fine-Fragrance Studies
- **Biological Basis of Aesthetics**: the authors relate floral and animalic preferences to biological history and learning. These are period interpretations, not proof of universal liking, human pheromone effects, or a fixed gender for an odor.
- **Descent of Perfumes**: recognize families and transformations of earlier structures. Comparative study asks which relation remains and what changed; it is more informative than memorizing a marketing note pyramid.
- **Selected Great Perfumes**: classics provide compositional case studies across floral, chypre, amber, green, and other forms. A named perfume must be tied to the version discussed. The historical comparison cannot certify a contemporary bottle's ingredients.

### Chapters 13–16: Functional Products, New Materials, Constraints, Market
- **Functional-product performance**: the substrate and product chemistry constrain the perfumer. Assess odor in the container, during use, and on the target surface afterward. A material must reach that surface, survive the application, remain there, and produce perceptible headspace.
- **New materials**: evaluate what a material permits in an accord and in the application, rather than assuming novelty or isolated attractiveness makes it useful. Potency, diffusion, persistence, stability, and cost interact.
- **Constraints to Creation**: supply, price, compounding practicality, safety, environmental concerns, color, packaging, and stability narrow the workable palette. Current decisions require current data; historical examples are not compliance clearance.
- **Perfumer and market**: consumer expectations help define success, but technical description, preference, and market acceptance answer different questions. Keep a descriptive test separate from a liking test and from the decision to commercialize.

### Chapters 17–20: Scientific Fundamentals
- **Chemical structure and reactions**: functional groups, isomers, and reactions such as oxidation or hydrolysis can affect odor and product behavior. Similar names are not chemical identity; aldehydic as an odor descriptor is not synonymous with the aldehyde functional group. Confirm exact material identity before substitution.
- **Physical basis**: volatility concerns entry into the vapor phase; substantivity concerns persistence on a particular substrate. Solvent affinity, partitioning, and material interactions change performance. Do not predict the whole evaporation curve solely from molecular mass or neat-material boiling point.
- **Thresholds**: air and water thresholds are not interchangeable; published values differ by method, observer, and medium. Detecting an odor and recognizing it are different achievements. An old threshold table is not a universal dosing schedule.
- **Odor value (OV)**: the concentration-to-threshold ratio helps think about potential odor relevance. It is not a direct linear scale of perceived intensity. The authors discuss **Stevens's law** and differing intensity/concentration slopes, explicitly recognizing empirical and method-dependent limits. A fixed multiplier cannot predict perceived strength across all materials and mixtures.

## Worked Example
The authors develop a carnation direction from a two-material relation. First compare eugenol and benzyl salicylate at several proportions, looking for the combination's character rather than a mathematical midpoint. Add ylang as a third contributor and compare whether the result becomes a coherent spicy floral. Then test whether that character survives incorporation into a larger perfume, as in their discussion of *L'Air du Temps*. This reconstructs the learning sequence; it does not disclose that perfume's formula or endorse historical concentrations for skin use.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When a blend is loud but shapeless, identify the organizing accord and test subtraction before adding another attractive note.
- When it fades for one observer, check adaptation and material-specific sensitivity before increasing the dose.
- When it lasts but does not project, assess headspace and substrate retention separately; adding more tenacious material may leave the distinction unresolved.
- When an attempted match drifts, return to the simplest supported skeleton and compare the whole after a rest.
- When transferring an alcoholic perfume into a functional product, repeat application and stability evaluation rather than preserving the formula by default.
- When using a base, account for its overlapping constituents and its effect on reproducibility, cost, and the visible formula.
- When a numerical prediction depends on an unknown threshold, slope, or medium, state that dependency and use a controlled comparison.

## Key Takeaways
1. Balance is perceptual and relational, not equal mass.
2. Identity, projection, persistence, and stability deserve separate observations.
3. Classification and analytical matching guide experiments; they do not replace them.
4. Old formulas and scientific generalizations need source and currency boundaries.
